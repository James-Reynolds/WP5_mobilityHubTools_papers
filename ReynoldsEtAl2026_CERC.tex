  \begin{abstract}
  Mobility hubs are an emerging approach that often involves colocating
  shared bikes, scooters, cars and/or other services with transit.
  Together these might to provide an attractive alternative to car
  ownership, or otherwise support more sustainable travel behaviours. A
  range of signage, information displays and branding appproaches have
  already been developed by practitioners, but best practices are
  unclear. As well, tools to help practitioners, tactical urbanists or
  others define their own mobility hub out of existing co-located
  services appear to be lacking. This paper reports the development
  basic tools for creating maps and a informational poster, totem or
  other display for mobility hubs in any location. Are there multiple
  mobility offerings in close proximity in your community? These tools,
  a printer and somewhere hang a poster might be all that is needed to
  spread the word.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Mobility hubs might help in shifting towards more walking, cycling and
transit-based travel through providing options for those, more
occasional, trips that are otherwise difficult without a car. They often
involve implementing facilities for shared cars, bikes and other
micro-mobility vehicles in close proximity to transit and/or with
consistent branding, informational signs and other measures.\\
However, in many locations shared cars and micro-mobility services,
transit and other facilities such as bike parking are already in close
proximity. Such offerings may already be well known to users or
surrounding communities, but might be convertable into a `mobility hub'
with minimal signage and information.

As yet there does not appear to be a consistent approach to mobility hub
branding and informational displays.\\
Various implementation agencies appear to have created signs, maps and
similar elements independently. What might represent best practices is
unclear.\\
As well, the graphic design, mapping and similar needed to develop such
signs for each site may limit the ability for practitioners to keep
information up to date, or for bottom-up implementation, trials or other
low-cost mobility hub creation efforts.

This paper reports findings from case and design-based research seeking
to develop new tools to support low-cost mobility hub implementation.
The rest of this paper is structured as follows: the next section
outlines the context for this study. The research methodology is then
described, followed by presentation of the results. These are then
briefly discussed prior to a conclusion that includes identification of
directions for future research.

\section{Research context}\label{research-context}

Shared bike, scooter and other micro-mobility systems have been widely
introduced to transport systems over the last couple of decades. These
may help to increase mobility and sustainability but have not been
entirely without problems. Inappropriate parking of dockless vehicles,
environmental and other impacts associated with rebalancing and battery
swapping, the risk of fires at recharging facilities, safety challenges
and other issues have been identified (see for example
\citet{Meigs:2023aa}, \citet{Zhang:2022aa}, \citet{Bozzi:2021aa}).

The emerging `mobility hub' concept may help towards limiting such
issues and integrating shared mobility services into transport
systems.\\
Mobility hubs were part of ``The Big Move'' transport plan in Toronto,
where the aim was to intensify landuse, facilitate modal transfers and
otherwise develop major regional transit stations
\citep[\citet{Metrolinx:2008ab}, \citet{Salsberg:2010aa},
\citet{Engel-Yan:2012aa}]{Metrolinx:2008aa}. \citet{Roukouni:2023aa},
however, describes how the research literature has extended on this to
define `shared mobility hubs' in terms of seamless integration of shared
modes, sometimes including transit, and local facilities and
services\footnote{ \emph{``A shared mobility hub is a place where
  different transport modes are integrated seamlessly, promoting
  efficient and sustainable urban mobility. Emphasis is given on shared
  mobility options such as bikes, scooters and cars. Connection with
  public transport (transit) is desirable but not necessary. The
  integration between mobility suppliers is important to provide a
  seamless, flexible connection at these transfer points. Ensuring an
  enjoyable experience for travelers is a crucial part of the hub
  concept as well, and therefore the hub should be considered not just
  as a transfer node but as a place where a variety of services come
  together, such as logistics services or restaurants''} (Roukouni et
  al.~2023).}. Shared \emph{electric} mobility hubs, such as those
currently being trialed in Ireland as part of the ROBUST project
\citep{ROBUST:2025aa}, may represent a further development through
providing power connections for vehicle recharging to dedicated on-site
parking space, thereby eliminating the need for rebalancing or battery
swapping.

Signage, branding, maps and other forms of public and user information
at mobility hubs is likely to be of high importance for achieving the
``integration'' and ``seamless, flexible connection'' when implementing
mobility hubs.\\
Basic signage, linemarking or similar would appear likely to be
relatively easy for many practitioners to implement within their own
institutional setting. What may be lacking, however, are tools and
standarised approaches for mobility hub information provision based on
current best practices that can be quickly and easily adapted to local
contexts. As well, there may be a need for information displays to be
refreshed if further offers are added to a mobility hub, or as the
surrounding urban environment changes over time. People, solutions and
tools tend to move on once decisions emerge from a policy-making
effort\footnote{c.f. the `Garbage Can' Model. See \citet{Lyles:1988aa},
  \citet{Parsons:1995aa} and others}, which may lead to challenges for
practitioners tasked with updates and maintenance.

As well, political, public and institutional opposition can be
challenging for implementing even small changes to urban environments.
Bottom-up and incremental approaches, `pop-ups' and more formal trials
have proven to be successful and pragmatic strategies for building
legitimacy \emph{through} implementation \citep{Reynolds:2021ab}.
However, these might rely on rapid prototyping, iterative co-design or
other low-cost production of information displays and signage.\\
Likewise, `Movement and Place' and `Complete Streets'
\citep{Delbosc:2018aa}, and tactical urbanism approaches
\citep{Lydon:2012aa, Lydon:2015aa, Davidson:2013aa, Blumgart:2016ab, Morhayim:2012aa, Longhurst:2015aa}
suggest way to challenge prevailing naratives about road space
allocation, give communities greater power over (their own) urban
environments, and demonstrate the potential of change.\\
For practitioners, tactical urbanists and others to adopt a `do it
yourself' approach to mobility hub creation (especially when multiple
services are already provided within close proximity, and a `mobility
hub' might be made with no engingeering works), there may the need for
generalised tools to support mapping, signage creation and other sources
of information.\\
As of yet, there does not appear to be a clear understanding of `best
practices' in mobility hub signage and information provision, or tools
to quickly and easily produce, maps, analysis and interpretations that
would support the definition of a place as a hub and communication of
its existing mobility and other offerings. The next section, therefore
describes the case- and design-based methodology adopted in this study
for the purposes of filling this gap.

\section{Methodology}\label{methodology}

This research aims to improve understanding of `best practices' in in
mobility hub signage and information provision, and to develop tools to
support mobility hub implementation. Research questions relate to:
\emph{how} information is provided at and about mobility hubs?
\emph{how} information at and about mobility hubs might ideally be
provided? (i.e.~best practices) and \emph{how} can such best practices
be made generally applicable?*\footnote{More formally, the (null)
  hypothesis tested in this study is that it is \emph{not} possible to
  develop software tools for generating mobility hub information
  displays that reflect best practices and are generally applicable to
  other sites.}

\subsection{Study design}\label{study-design}

The study designed adopts the Double Diamond design process
\citep{UK-Design-Council:0aa}. \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab} identify this as a suitable tool for design-based
research seeking to develop solutions to mobility problems. The Double
Diamond process involves iteration across four phases: 1.
\emph{Discovery}, 2. \emph{Definition}, 3. \emph{Development} and 4.
\emph{Delivery}. These phases alternate between divergent (1 \& 3) and
convergent (2 \& 4) approaches, although the overall process is
iterative and may involve multiple passes through each phase.

Here the \emph{Discovery} phase utilised a case research approach, which
\citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab} and others identify as
being especially effective for when: theory development is at an early
stage; research questions relate to `how' or `why'; and when exploring
phenomena within real-world contexts.

\subsection{Participants, Material and
Instruments}\label{participants-material-and-instruments}

The authors were the only participants in the research reported in this
paper. As discussed in the conclusion to this paper further research as
part of the ROBUST project is expected to involve mobility hub users,
stakeholders and others in testing, co-design and continued iteration.
These, however, are beyond the scope of this paper.

Material for the study was drawn from: photos taken during sites visits
made by the lead author in the \emph{Discovery} phase; and mapping,
imagery and other online resources. Open Street Map datasets provided an
input for the \emph{Development} phase.

Instruments included synthesis tables for cross-case comparisons. These
were developed as per \citet{Miles:1994aa}. \emph{Development} used the
R programming language to replicate the informational display styles
discovered at the leading cases (Bristol and Birmingham). Package
development during the \emph{Delivery} phase was guided by
\citet{wickham2023r}.

\subsection{Procedure and analysis}\label{procedure-and-analysis}

In the \emph{Discovery} phase cases were selected from the larger sample
available at
https://www.como.org.uk/mobility-hubs/built-andplanned-hubs. Inclusion
was somewhat constrained by logistics, but guided by the case research
methodological literature (see \citet{Eisenhardt:1989aa},
\citet{Stuart:2002aa}, \citet{Voss:2002aa}, \citet{Eisenhardt:2007aa},
\citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Barratt:2011aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab} and
others). Selected cases included the leading examples of the mobility
hubs developed by the West of England Mayoral Combined Authority (WEMCA,
Bristol) and Transport for West Midlands (Birmingham). More
representative examples from Camden Council (London) and the London
Borough of Redbridge (Woodford and Wanstead) were also included to
provide polar opposites for cross-case comparison. Within each case a
small number of mobility hubs were visited to allow for within-case
comparison\footnote{\emph{Redbridge}: South Woodford and High Street,
  Wanstead; and in \emph{Camden}: Goldington Crescent, and Doric Way;
  \emph{Bristol}: Abbey Wood, UWE Frenchay, and Gainsborough Square.
  \emph{Birmingham}: Huntingtree Park and Andrew Road}.

The \emph{Definition} phase involved synthesis of findings from the site
visits and about each included case. As per
\citet{UK-Design-Council:0aa}{]}, \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab}, this provided the basis for defining the problem
that needs to be addressed by the research outputs.

The \emph{Development} phase involved iterative software coding to first
replicate the Bristol and Birmingham style of information displays. This
software was then applied to contexts of the ROBUST hubs in Galway,
Letterkenny and Dundrum. Finally, a combined version that might be a
step towards best practices was developed for the \emph{Delivery} phase.
This final phase involved convergence on a combined display style based
on the synthesis of industry practices and responding to the problem
developed in the \emph{Definition} phase.\\
As per \citet{UK-Design-Council:0aa}{]}, \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab}, the research also included iteration through all
four phases. These iterations were tracked using the Git version control
system\footnote{Code used in the production of this paper is available
  at
  \url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/WP5_mobilityHubTools_papers/blob/main/ReynoldsEtAl2026_CERC.Rmd},
  which includes the \emph{Delivered} software functions at the time of
  final submission. The software package itself is available at
  \url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/mobilityHubTools}, but is
  expected to be developed further, so may change in the future. The
  last commit at the time of final submission is
  \href{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/mobilityHubTools/commit/a4fbbbe21c5eb63640f3e9d3cc5b548237c14b19}{a4fbbbe},
  dated August 6 2026.}. However, in the interests of clarity the next
section presents the results in a linear format, stepping through each
phase in turn.

Information displays varied between the small placard signs in South
Woodford and Wanstead (Redbridge, London) to the comprehensive totems /
pedestals in Bristol and Birmingham. Mobility hubs in Camden lacked any
information displays, although there was a nearby totem for the bike
share docking system at Doric Way (Figure \ref{fig:selected_images}, top
left.). The sites themselves were labelled as ``Mobility Hub'' and
``Local Travel Point'' in Birmingham, but lacked any form of naming in
Camden while the Bristol sites only showed the name of the locality
(Figure \ref{fig:bristol_images_panels}).

Totems all included two maps: one at a local scale (within 5 minutes
walk) and the second showing a large area (within 15 minutes). Hub
facilities were typically explictly listed or described on the totems,
but not included in mapping. Linemarking was commonly used to highlight
some hub components. The Bristol hubs included cube shaped indicators
mounted on poles above hub components (see Figure
\ref{fig:bristol_images_panels}, bottom right).

Where present, nearby transit services and facilities where typically
detailed on the information displays although this was not always the
case. Insecure bike parking (i.e.~locking hoops) was the most commonly
provided feature, although some hubs in Camden, Bristol and Birmingham
also included bicycle lockers or other limited-access facilities.
Seating and nearby toilets were available as part of some hubs, but no
hubs included comprehensive shelter or access to water. The Bristol hubs
included USB connections for phone recharging, and wifi internet
connectivity at some locations.

\subsection{Definition}\label{definition}

Findings during the \emph{Discovery} phase suggest that current
practices relating to information displays at mobility hubs are
inconsistent. The \emph{totems} in Birmingham, Birmingham and Doric Way
perhaps demonstrate advantages of having maps and hub details displayed
in large size. The small placard signs and the lack information at most
hubs in Camden provide contrasting examples where it may be unclear what
facilities are provided at the hubs or nearby.

The South Woodford placard (Figure \ref{fig:selected_images}, bottom
left) provides an example of \emph{limited information about what is
provided} or \emph{where it is} in relation to the hub. Only left or
right direction indications are provided to nearby transit, taxis and
shared cars. However, finding hub elements does not necessarily appear
easy when maps are included on totems. Both the Bristol and Birmingham
style of maps were at too large a scale for the precise location of all
of the various hub offerings to be shown.

At some hubs space that had originally been allocated to shared
micro-mobility had been taken over for other purposes. Changes to the
facilities and nearby services over time also meant that the original
hub information displays were out of date at some sites. Consistency and
the need to update information displays to reflect changing
circumstances would therefore appear to be challenges facing mobility
hub implementers and maintainers.

Therefore, overall the problem to be addressed in this design-based
research is to develop tools that might allow practitioners to: 1)
produce totem or poster-sized information displays; 2) that clearly show
hubs, surrounding facilities and services, and their location; and 3)
that can be generated rapidly, cheaply and otherwise updated over time
to reflect changing circumstances.

Based on the cases visited during the \emph{Discovery} phase there
appears to be five areas mobility hub information displays need to
address. Firstly, there is a need to identify what it is (e.g.~Mobility
Hub, Local Travel Point, Public Space). Secondly,

\subsection{Development}\label{development}

\begin{figure}

{\centering \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{graphics/bristol_and_birmingham_output}

\caption{Bristol hubs, selected images. Source: authors.}\label{fig:bristol_and_birmingham_output}

The \emph{Development} phase started with creation of software tools to
download Open Street Maps data and use it to replicate the Bristol and
Birmingham totems, as shown in Figure
\ref{fig:bristol_and_birmingham_output}. Each style includes
user-adjustable hub names, facility icons and directional indicators at
the top. Maps is generated for each hub at the local and regional
scales, indicating the location of nearby transit stops and stations,
and highlighting buildings and other features (see detail, Figure
\ref{fig:bristol_and_birmingham_output} bottom left). Icon and feature
locations are generated directly from the OSM datasets, although the
user can select which are to be highlighted and labeled. Finally, space
is allocated for facility description text and logos.

Further \emph{Development} involved creation of a \emph{synthesis}
display format, which responds to the problem \emph{Definition}. by: 1)
adopting an A0 poster size, so as to fit with typical plotter

\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{discussion-and-conclusions}

This design-based research has developed basic software tools that might
be used in the context of mobility hubs. The general format and styling
is largely based on elements in place at various mobility hubs in
Bristol, but as discussed above the flexibility of R might allow users
to adjust layouts, styling and other details as desired. As well as
being freely available, the OSM online platform allows this dataset to
be edited by anyone, including for the purposes of mobility hub
implementation Clearly, there are many avenues for further research,
including: expansion to produce websites, posters, banners, leaflets and
other informational products; developing more generic materials for
other purposes such as maintenance schedules, inspection logs,
management resources and other items that might help a mobility hub
`product owner' facilitate operations, continuance and growth. Further
development of `best practices' in mobility hub informational provision
might involve additional case studies, especially of non-UK sites, as
well as improvements to the base `hub signage mockup' developed here.
There may be a need to better understand what facilities and services
might best be included as `part' of a hub, as opposed to those that are
mapped as being `nearby'. More immediately, user testing and further
development of the software, and of the software outputs, especially the
maps, would seem an important direction for further research efforts in
the short term. This paper's title asked a question related to the
applicability of tactical urbanist approaches to the topic of mobility
hubs. Underlying this may be larger questions related to `what is a
mobility hub' and `who gets to define and advertise something as being a
mobility hub'. Perhaps every railway station, bus or tram stop, shared
or micro- mobility facilities or many other places are already mobility
hubs, but just no one has put up a sign or handed out a leaflet that
designates this as being the case? With access to this software and a
printer, turning the existing mobility offerings in your own community
into a defined hub may be within easier reach.
