  \begin{abstract}
  Mobility hubs involve co-locating shared bikes/e-bikes, cars,
  e-scooters or similar modes. They are often close to transit and other
  facilities, such as charging stations or parcel lockers, with the aim
  being to provide alternatives to (petrol-driven) car ownership and
  support more sustainable travel behaviours. A range of signage,
  information displays and branding appproaches for mobility hubs have
  already been developed by practitioners, but best practices are
  unclear. As well, tools to help create mobility hubs out of existing
  (and already co-located) services appear to be lacking. This paper
  reports a synthesis of existing practices, and development of software
  tools for creating maps and informational displays for mobility hubs
  in any location. Are there multiple mobility offerings in close

  a poster might be all that is needed to spread the word.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Shared mobility hubs are places where a range of modes and sometimes
other facilities are provided to support more sustainable travel. They
often include shared electric cars, bikes, cargobikes, e-bikes,
e-scooters or other micro-mobility services. Together, these might help
support shifts towards more walking and cycling, transit use, and
otherwise improve the sustainability of transport systems through
providing options for those (more occasional) trips that are difficult
without a private car.

Many mobility hubs have already been implemented, but with signage, maps
and other informational material created by individual agencies on a
site-by-site basis. Current and best practices are unclear. As well, the
graphic design, mapping and similar effort needed to develop such signs
for each site may limit the ability for practitioners to keep
information up to date, trial new hubs, or use bottom-up implementation
approaches to create hubs where shared or other modes are already in
close proximity.

This paper aims to explore and better understand mobility hub
information provision, and to develop tools to support practitioners. It
reports findings from case research examining existing practices at a
selection of mobility hubs. Also reported are findings from design-based
research that develops software tools for producing maps and information
displays for mobility hubs, based on both replication and synthesis of
industry practices. The rest of this paper is structured as follows: the
next section outlines the context for this study. The research
methodology is then described, followed by presentation of the results.
These are then briefly discussed prior to a conclusion that includes
identification of directions for future research.

\section{Research context}\label{research-context}

An early version of the mobility hub concept was developed in Toronto's
``Big Move'' transport plan \citep{Metrolinx:2008aa} in the late 2000s,
which used to the term\\
to denote selected major regional transit stations around which landuses
would be intensified and where intermodal transfers would be facilitated
\citep[\citet{Engel-Yan:2012aa}]{Salsberg:2010aa}. In the intervening
years there has been rapid developments in shared micro-mobility and
vehicle electrification, such that shared bike, e-bike and e-scooter
systems are common. Various problems have emerged, however, including
inappropriate parking of dockless vehicles blocking footpath; the
environmental and other impacts associated with rebalancing and battery
swapping; the risk of fires at recharging facilities; and safety
challenges associated with user behaviour (see for example,
\citet{Meigs:2023aa}, \citet{Zhang:2022aa} and \citet{Bozzi:2021aa}).
Consolidating shared and micro-mobility services into mobility hubs may
help to resolve some of these challenges.\\
Shared \emph{electric} mobility hubs where each shared e-car, e-bike or
other vehicle has its own powered parking space might eliminate the need
for rebalancing or battery swapping entirely. This is case at sites in
Galway and other parts of Ireland that are part of the current ongoing
Electric shaRed mOBility hUbS Trial (ROBUST) \citep{ROBUST:2025aa}, of
which the research described in this paper is a part.

Mobility hubs, however, appear likely to continue to come in many forms,
and incorporate a wide range of vehicles, facilities and other services.
Some guidance is provided by a definition recently developed from a
review of the literature, that highlights the need for ``integration'',
``seamless, flexible connection'' and ``\ldots an enjoyable experience
for travelers'' \citep{Roukouni:2023aa}. How such integration, user
experience and seamlessness might be achieved, implemented and
maintained by practitioners appears unclear.

Signs, maps and information displays would seem to be and important
consideration when creating a new mobility hub (whatever its form). More
broadly, `Movement and Place' and `Complete Streets'
\citep{Delbosc:2018aa}, and tactical urbanism approaches
{[}\citet{Lydon:2012aa}; \citet{Lydon:2015aa}; \citet{Davidson:2013aa};
\citet{Blumgart:2016ab}; \citet{Morhayim:2012aa}; Longhurst:2015aa{]}
suggest way to challenge prevailing naratives about road space
allocation, give communities greater power over (their own) urban
environments, and demonstrate the potential of change. Bottom-up and
incremental approaches, `pop-ups' and more formal trials have also been
found to be pragmatic strategies for building legitimacy \emph{through}
implementation \citep{Reynolds:2021ab}.\\
In the context of mobility hubs, however, these might rely on rapid
prototyping, iterative co-design or other approaches for which
practitioners, tactical urbanists and others might to need adopt a\\
`do it yourself' attitude when it comes to developing information
displays and supporting maps. As of yet, there does not appear to be
standarised approaches for providing such information, or a clear
understanding of best practices. Nor are there tools that might allow\\
such best practices to be quickly and easily adapted to local contexts,
and for updates to be made as a mobility hub or its surrounding urban
environment changes over time. The next section, therefore describes the
case- and design-based research methodology adopted in this study to (at
least start) filling this gap in knowledge.

\section{Methodology}\label{methodology}

The research described in this paper is part of the larger ROBUST
project on shared electric mobility hubs, which was briefly discussed
above.\\
This component of the project aims to improve understanding of `best
practices' in in mobility hub signage and information provision, and to
develop tools to support mobility hub implementation. Research questions
relate to: \emph{how} information is provided at and about mobility
hubs? \emph{how} information at and about mobility hubs might ideally be
provided? (i.e.~best practices) and \emph{how} can such best practices
be made generally applicable?*\footnote{More formally, the (null)
  hypothesis tested in this study is that it is \emph{not} possible to
  develop software tools for generating mobility hub information
  displays that reflect best practices and are generally applicable to
  other sites.}

\subsection{Study design}\label{study-design}

The study designed adopts the Double Diamond design process
\citep{UK-Design-Council:0aa}. \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab} identify this as a suitable tool for design-based
research seeking to develop solutions to mobility problems. The Double
Diamond process involves iteration across four phases: 1.
\emph{Discovery}, 2. \emph{Definition}, 3. \emph{Development} and 4.
\emph{Delivery}. These phases alternate between divergent (1 \& 3) and
convergent (2 \& 4) approaches, although the overall process is
iterative and may involve multiple passes through each phase.

Here the \emph{Discovery} and \emph{Development} phases utilised a case
research approach, which \citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab}
and others identify as being especially effective for when: theory
development is at an early stage; research questions relate to `how' or
`why'; and when exploring phenomena within real-world contexts.

\subsection{Participants, Material and
Instruments}\label{participants-material-and-instruments}

The authors were the only participants in the research reported in this
paper. As discussed in the conclusion to this paper further research as
part of the ROBUST project is expected to involve mobility hub users,
stakeholders and others in testing, co-design and continued iteration.
These, however, are beyond the scope of this paper.

Material for the study was drawn from: photos taken during sites visits
made by the lead author in the \emph{Discovery} phase; and mapping,
imagery and other online resources. Open Street Map datasets provided an
input for the \emph{Development} phase.

Instruments included synthesis tables for cross-case comparisons. These
were developed as per \citet{Miles:1994aa}. \emph{Development} used the
R programming language to replicate the informational display styles
discovered at the leading cases (Bristol and Birmingham). Package
development during the \emph{Delivery} phase was guided by
\citet{wickham2023r}.

\subsection{Procedure and analysis}\label{procedure-and-analysis}

In the \emph{Discovery} phase cases were selected from the larger sample
available at
https://www.como.org.uk/mobility-hubs/built-andplanned-hubs. Inclusion
was somewhat constrained by logistics, but guided by the case research
methodological literature (see \citet{Eisenhardt:1989aa},
\citet{Stuart:2002aa}, \citet{Voss:2002aa}, \citet{Eisenhardt:2007aa},
\citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Barratt:2011aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab} and
others). Selected cases included the leading examples of the mobility
hubs developed by the West of England Mayoral Combined Authority (WEMCA,
Bristol) and Transport for West Midlands (Birmingham). More
representative examples from Camden Council (London) and the London
Borough of Redbridge (Woodford and Wanstead) were also included to
provide polar opposites for cross-case comparison. Within each case a
small number of mobility hubs were visited to allow for within-case
comparison\footnote{\emph{Redbridge}: South Woodford and High Street,
  Wanstead; and in \emph{Camden}: Goldington Crescent, and Doric Way;
  \emph{Bristol}: Abbey Wood, UWE Frenchay, and Gainsborough Square.
  \emph{Birmingham}: Huntingtree Park and Andrew Road}.

The \emph{Definition} phase involved synthesis of findings from the site
visits and about each included case. As per
\citet{UK-Design-Council:0aa}{]}, \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab}, this provided the basis for defining the problem
that needs to be addressed by the research outputs.

The \emph{Development} phase involved iterative software coding to first
replicate the Bristol and Birmingham style of information displays. This
replication was first undertaken for cases included in the
\emph{Discovery}, and then repeated to test the software on the contexts
of the ROBUST hubs in Galway, Letterkenny and Dundrum. Finally, a
combined display style was developed based on the synthesis of industry
practices undertaken during the \emph{Discovery} phase and responding to
the problem developed in the \emph{Definition} phase. As per
\citet{UK-Design-Council:0aa}{]}, \citet{Coxon:2018aa} and
\citet{Coxon:2021ab}, the research also included iteration through all
four phases\footnote{ These iterations were tracked using the Git
  version control system, and code used in the production of this paper
  is available at
  \url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/WP5_mobilityHubTools_papers/blob/main/ReynoldsEtAl2026_CERC.Rmd}.
  The software package itself is available at
  \url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/mobilityHubTools}, and is
  expected to be developed further during later parts of the ROBUST
  project}. However, in the interests of clarity the next section
presents the results in a linear format, stepping through each phase in
turn.

Information displays ranged from small placard signs (South Woodford and
Wanstead) to comprehensive totem displays and posters (Bristol and
Birmingham). Mobility hubs in Camden lacked any information signs. Sites
were labelled as ``Mobility Hub'' or ``Village Hub'' (South Woodford and
Wanstead) and ``Local Travel Point'' in Birmingham.

Where present, totems all included two maps: one at a local scale
(within 5 minutes walk) and a second showing a larger area (within 15
minutes). These generally showed nearby transit, cafes, shops and other
points of interest.\\
Text descriptions of local walking and cycling routes, hub facilities
and transit services were also included in the information displays in
Bristol and Birmingham.

Many of the sites had linemarking that highlighted some hub components.
The Bristol hubs also included cube shaped indicators mounted on poles\\
to help with finding hub components. (see Figure
\ref{fig:bristol_images_panels}, bottom right).

Bicycle parking hoops were the most commonly provided feature. Some hubs
in Camden, Bristol and Birmingham also included bicycle lockers or
similar higher security parking. Seating and nearby toilets were
available as part of some hubs, but no hubs included comprehensive
shelter or access to water. The Bristol hubs included USB connections
for phone recharging, and wifi internet connectivity at some locations.

Overall, findings during the \emph{Discovery} phase suggest that current
practices relating to information displays at mobility hubs are
inconsistent. Best practices appear to be represented by the totem
displays in Bristol and Birmingham. These are generally similiar to
other such displays, notably the Transport For London Cycles display
that is close to, but not part of, the mobility hub in Doric Way, Camden
(Figure \ref{fig:selected_images}, top left).

Also shown is an assessment of how each of the cases does (or does not)
respond to each.

The first question shown in Table \ref{tab:definition_details} relates
to identifying a mobility hub as something in and of itself. Various
terms were used in practice (Village Hub, Public Space, Local Travel
Point), except in Camden (no information display) and Bristol (place
name only).

The second question (\emph{Where am I?}) was answered in the cases
either by place names (Redbridge, Bristol and Birmingham) or not at all
(Camden). For the third question (\emph{What is here?}) the best
industry practices appear to be identify hub elements through text
descriptions, icons, linemarking and cube markers. Notably, hub elements
were not always shown on the maps answering the fourth question
(\emph{what else is nearby?}). Answers to the final question shown in
Table \ref{tab:definition_details}(\emph{How can you travel from here?})
were similarly limited, although some information displays included text
descriptions and limited mapping.

Overall, therefore the \emph{Defined} problem to be addressed by this
research related to produce information displays that answer these five
questions. Hence, the final two Double Diamond phases involved
developing and delivering software tools that could create such displays
in a totem or poster-sized format. Such tools also needed to include
functionality to assist users in obtaining data and producing maps, but
also allow user input of component elements, descriptive text and other
details about a mobility hub.\\
Finally, the software needed to be generalisable, such that it might be
used to develop mobility hub informational displays elsewhere
(i.e.~beyond the cases examined in this study).

The synthesis totem answers the five questions developed in the
\emph{Definition} phase of this research, as indicated in Figure

\subsection{Delivery}\label{delivery}

Software developed in this research was assembled into a package of
functions, as per the guidance provided by \citet{wickham2023r}. This
package is available at
\url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/mobilityHubTools}. It includes
functions for creating maps (e.g.~bristol\_local\_map.R,
synthesis\_hub\_elements\_map.R) and totems
(e.g.~brimingham\_totem\_pedestal.R, synthesis\_totem.R). As per the
\citet{wickham2023r} recommendations each function includes a
description of variables and other details (through a Roxygen Skeleton),
such that R help files and other package documentation are automatically
generated.\\
Also included in the package are icons (.svg) and test data used to
generate the Gainsborough Square outputs.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}\label{discussion-and-conclusions}

As briefly noted earlier, the research described in this paper is part
of the ROBUST project, which involves trials of shared electric mobility
hubs in various locations across Ireland. Part of this larger program of
research will include looking at user behaviour, usability and related
issues under real-world conditions. Experiments with information
displays, including testing and co-design with users, is a direction for
future research during ROBUST. The research described in this paper,
however, represents an initial exploration of this topic both through
case studies of current practices and use of a design-research approach.

Hub characteristics discovered from the cases studied during the first
phase of this research suggest mobility hubs come in many forms. There
also appears to be much variability in information displays and user
guidance, from comprehensive displays in Bristol and Birmingham to
linemarking only at some sites in London. The design problem this study
has focused on relates specifically to totems and mapping, but clearly
there are opportunities for further research into mobility hub
wayfinding and information provision. As well, even those mobility hubs
included within the cases with more extensive information displays do
not appear to have fully answered the five questions defined here as the
design problem to be addressed.

While the synthesis totem display output from the \emph{Development}
phase of the research described here does answer all five questions
(multiple times), further design improvements can clearly be made. There
may be a need to better understand what facilities and services might
best be included as `part' of a hub, as opposed to those that are mapped
as being `nearby'. More immediately, further development of the software
and user testing of the software outputs would seem a direction for
future research.

However, the aim of this research was to improve understandings related
to mobility hub informational displays, and explore whether it was
possible to develop software tools for implementing mobility hubs at
other locations. The maps and other details shown in Figure
\ref{ref:display_synthesis_details} and three different outputs for the
Galway ROBUST hub\footnote{See
  https://github.com/James-Reynolds/WP5\_mobilityHubTools\_papers/tree/main/results/galway\_synthesis\_test.pdf}
indicate that it is possible to use OSM data and mapping tiles to
produce mobility hub information displays that might be suitable for
prototyping, public consultation or to hand to a graphic
designer\footnote{More formally, disproving the (null) hypothesis this
  paper tests.}. The flexibility of R might also allow users to adjust
layouts, styling and other details as desired. Likewise, the OSM dataset
can be freely accessed and edited by anyone. Hence, starting with the
developed software and a printer tactical urbanists or other grassroots
activitist might be able define a mobility hub in their own community,
and keep information displays up to date as circumstances change. This
might raise larger questions, such as \emph{who gets to decide what a
mobility hub is?} Perhaps mobility hubs already exist wherever there are
combinations of: railway or electric vehicle charging stations; bus or
tram stops; shared bike docks, parking zones or other micro-mobility
offerings; or other transport-related facilities and services. Hopefully
the software tools developed in this study may help facilitate bottom-up
implementation, pop-ups or trials, or other efforts to develop and
maintain mobility hubs.

More generally, the research outcomes of this study may provide insights
about current mobility hub information displays developed by
practitioners, as a step towards identifying `best practices'.\\
Mobility hubs are still a new and evolving approach. As per the
\citet{Roukouni:2023aa} definition, a mobility hub needs to provide
``integration'', ``seamless, flexible connection'' and ``\ldots an
enjoyable experience for travelers'' across multiple modes, while also
connecting users to surrounding services and facilities.\\
Overall, it appears that there is some way to go in understanding how to
deliver upon this, even within the narrower confines of information
displays and other guidance for users at mobility hubs.
