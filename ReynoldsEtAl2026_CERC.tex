  \begin{abstract}
  Mobility hubs are an emerging approach that often involves colocating
  shared bikes, scooters, cars and/or other services with transit.
  Together these might to provide an attractive alternative to car
  ownership, or otherwise support more sustainable travel behaviours. A
  range of signage, information displays and branding appproaches have
  already been developed by practitioners, but best practices are
  unclear. As well, tools to help practitioners, tactical urbanists or
  others define their own mobility hub out of existing co-located
  services appear to be lacking. This paper reports the development
  basic tools for creating maps and a informational poster, totem or
  other display for mobility hubs in any location. Are there multiple
  mobility offerings in close proximity in your community? These tools,
  a printer and somewhere hang a poster might be all that is needed to
  spread the word.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Mobility hubs might help in shifting towards more walking, cycling and
transit-based travel through providing options for those, more
occasional, trips that are otherwise difficult without a car. They often
involve implementing facilities for shared cars, bikes and other
micro-mobility vehicles in close proximity to transit and/or with
consistent branding, informational signs and other measures.\\
However, in many locations shared cars and micro-mobility services,
transit and other facilities such as bike parking are already in close
proximity. Such offerings may already be well known to users or
surrounding communities, but might be convertable into a `mobility hub'
with minimal signage and information.

As yet there does not appear to be a consistent approach to mobility hub
branding and informational displays.\\
Various implementation agencies appear to have created signs, maps and
similar elements independently. What might represent best practices is
unclear.\\
As well, the graphic design, mapping and similar needed to develop such
signs for each site may limit the ability for practitioners to keep
information up to date, or for bottom-up implementation, trials or other
low-cost mobility hub creation efforts.

This paper reports findings from case and design-based research seeking
to develop new tools to support low-cost mobility hub implementation.
The rest of this paper is structured as follows: the next section
outlines the context for this study. The research methodology is then
described, followed by presentation of the results. These are then
briefly discussed prior to a conclusion that includes identification of
directions for future research.

\section{Research context}\label{research-context}

Shared bike, scooter and other micro-mobility systems have been widely
introduced to transport systems over the last couple of decades. These
may help to increase mobility and sustainability but have not been
entirely without problems. Inappropriate parking of dockless vehicles,
environmental and other impacts associated with rebalancing and battery
swapping, the risk of fires at recharging facilities, safety challenges
and other issues have been identified (see for example
\citet{Meigs:2023aa}, \citet{Zhang:2022aa}, \citet{Bozzi:2021aa}).

The emerging `mobility hub' concept may help towards limiting such
issues and integrating shared mobility services into transport
systems.\\
Mobility hubs were part of ``The Big Move'' transport plan in Toronto,
where the aim was to intensify landuse, facilitate modal transfers and
otherwise develop major regional transit stations
\citep[\citet{Metrolinx:2008ab}, \citet{Salsberg:2010aa},
\citet{Engel-Yan:2012aa}]{Metrolinx:2008aa}. \citet{Roukouni:2023aa},
however, describes how the research literature has extended on this to
define `shared mobility hubs' in terms of seamless integration of shared
modes, sometimes including transit, and local facilities and
services\footnote{ \emph{``A shared mobility hub is a place where
  different transport modes are integrated seamlessly, promoting
  efficient and sustainable urban mobility. Emphasis is given on shared
  mobility options such as bikes, scooters and cars. Connection with
  public transport (transit) is desirable but not necessary. The
  integration between mobility suppliers is important to provide a
  seamless, flexible connection at these transfer points. Ensuring an
  enjoyable experience for travelers is a crucial part of the hub
  concept as well, and therefore the hub should be considered not just
  as a transfer node but as a place where a variety of services come
  together, such as logistics services or restaurants''} (Roukouni et
  al.~2023).}. Shared \emph{electric} mobility hubs, such as those
currently being trialed in Ireland as part of the ROBUST project
\citep{ROBUST:2025aa}, may represent a further development through
providing power connections for vehicle recharging to dedicated on-site
parking space, thereby eliminating the need for rebalancing or battery
swapping.

Signage, branding, maps and other forms of public and user information
at mobility hubs is likely to be of high importance for achieving the
``integration'' and ``seamless, flexible connection'' when implementing
mobility hubs.\\
Basic signage, linemarking or similar would appear likely to be
relatively easy for many practitioners to implement within their own
institutional setting. What may be lacking, however, are tools and
standarised approaches for mobility hub information provision based on
current best practices that can be quickly and easily adapted to local
contexts. As well, there may be a need for information displays to be
refreshed if further offers are added to a mobility hub, or as the
surrounding urban environment changes over time. People, solutions and
tools tend to move on once decisions emerge from a policy-making
effort\footnote{c.f. the `Garbage Can' Model. See \citet{Lyles:1988aa},
  \citet{Parsons:1995aa} and others}, which may lead to challenges for
practitioners tasked with updates and maintenance.

As well, political, public and institutional opposition can be
challenging for implementing even small changes to urban environments.
Bottom-up and incremental approaches, `pop-ups' and more formal trials
have proven to be successful and pragmatic strategies for building
legitimacy \emph{through} implementation \citep{Reynolds:2021ab}.
However, these might rely on rapid prototyping, iterative co-design or
other low-cost production of information displays and signage.\\
Likewise, `Movement and Place' and `Complete Streets'
\citep{Delbosc:2018aa}, and tactical urbanism approaches
\citep{Lydon:2012aa, Lydon:2015aa, Davidson:2013aa, Blumgart:2016ab, Morhayim:2012aa, Longhurst:2015aa}
suggest way to challenge prevailing naratives about road space
allocation, give communities greater power over (their own) urban
environments, and demonstrate the potential of change.\\
For practitioners, tactical urbanists and others to adopt a `do it
yourself' approach to mobility hub creation (especially when multiple
services are already provided within close proximity, and a `mobility
hub' might be made with no engingeering works), there may the need for
generalised tools to support mapping, signage creation and other sources
of information.\\
As of yet, there does not appear to be a clear understanding of `best
practices' in mobility hub signage and information provision, or tools
to quickly and equlity produce, maps, analysis and interpretations that
would support the definition of a place as a hub and communication of
its existing mobility and other offerings. Therefore, in the next
section this paper describes the case- and design-based methodology
adopted in this study for the purposes of filling this gap.

\section{Methodology}\label{methodology}

\subsection{Study design}\label{study-design}

This study is designed as per the Double Diamond design process
\citep{UK-Design-Council:0aa}, which \citet{Coxon:2018aa,Coxon:2021ab}
identify as a research tool applicable for influencing travel
behaviours, improving traveller experiences and developing solutions to
mobility problems. The Double Diamond involves four phases that
alternatively\\
diverge and converge. In the (diverging) \emph{Discovery} phase the
issue being researched is explored before (converging) on a
\emph{Definition} of the problem to be solved.\\
This is followed by (diverging) \emph{Development} and exploration of
options and then (covergence) on the \emph{Delivery} of design output,
which forms the research findings \citep{UK-Design-Council:0aa}. With
this being an iterative process, however, the findings reported in this
paper represent only an initial foray into mobility hub information
design and software development.

The \emph{Discovery} phase utilised a case research approach, as
\citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab} and others identify this as
being especially effective for when theory development is at an early
stage, for how and why research questions, and when exploring phenomena
within real-world contexts. Here the research question relates to
\emph{how do real-world mobility hub information provision practices
indicate user needs and best practices, and how can these be made
generically applicable?}\footnote{More formally, the (null) hypothesis
  tested in this study is that it is \emph{not} possible to develop
  software tools for generating mobility hub information displays that
  reflect best practices and are generally applicable to other sites.}

The methodological literature identifies various considerations for
undertaking case research that include: the need to be seeking
generalisable findings despite researching at depth within the case
contexts; a need to keep case numbers managable, with typically no more
than five to ten cases included; and a theoretically-driven approach to
case selection to include those that are leading, critical or usual
examples, or that might otherwise build new knowledge. Here
generalisability is addressed directly through the main research output
being software applicable to mobility hubs in any location, not just the
cases (see \citet{Eisenhardt:1989aa}, \citet{Stuart:2002aa},
\citet{Voss:2002aa}, \citet{Eisenhardt:2007aa},
\citet{Denscombe:2007aa}, \citet{Barratt:2011aa}, \citet{Yin:2018ab} and
others).

Cases were selected from a larger sample available at
https://www.como.org.uk/mobility-hubs/built-andplanned-hubs, with the
main unit of analysis being the locality. Selection was somewhat
constrained by logistics, but included the leading examples from the
West of England Mayoral Combined Authority (WEMCA, Bristol) and
Transport for West Midlands (Birmingham), more representative examples
from Camden Council (London) and the London Borough of Redbridge
(Woodford and Wanstead), so as to provide cross-comparison to polar
opposites.\\
Within each case, various mobility hubs were selected\footnote{\emph{Redbridge}:
  South Woodford and High Street, Wanstead; and in \emph{Camden}:
  Goldington Crescent, and Doric Way; \emph{Bristol}: Abbey Wood, UWE
  Frenchay, and Gainsborough Square. \emph{Birmingham}: Huntingtree Park
  and Andrew Road} as sub-units of analysis to allow within-case
comparisons. Case study questions provided prompts for field data
collection about site conditions, including the nature of information
provided to users.\\
These and collected data are shown in the synthesis tables included in
the results section later in the paper, which provided the mechanism for
the within and cross-cass comparisons that informed the research
findings and later \emph{Definition}, \emph{Development} and
\emph{Delivery} phases.

\subsection{Participants, Material and Instruments, Procedure and
analysis}\label{participants-material-and-instruments-procedure-and-analysis}

The authors were the only participants in the research reported in this
paper, working through the four Double Diamond phases as both designers
and (hypothetical) users. Sites were visited by the lead author. Further
iterations are expected to involve qualitative engagement with
stakeholders, co-design with users, and quantitative testing of design
outputs to refine understandings of the design problem (i.e.~user needs)
that mobility hub information provision should address, and what designs
might be most effective for doing so.

The methodological literature discusses the use of formal case study
questions and synthesis tables and other qualitative analysis tools (see
\citet{Miles:1994aa} and others cited above). These approaches informed
data collection during the \emph{Discovery} phase, with synthesis tables
based around case study questions providing the primary research
instruments.

\emph{Development} used the R programming language to first replicate
informational display styles discovered at the leading cases (Bristol
and Birmingham).\\
A combined version representing `best practices' was then generated
based on the design problem identified in the \emph{Definition} stage.
Package development during the \emph{Delivery} phase was guided by
\citet{wickham2023r}.

Information panels, maps and other guides to facilities and services
were present at some hubs, but notably not at those in London. This is
likely because of existing maps and other information at nearby tube
stations, bus stops or otherwise, which may have lessened the need for
mobility-hub-specific signage and mapping. Figure 1 (left) shows
selected images of informational panels and signage found at the hubs in
and Bristol, which shared some common design elements such as including
both local and area- wide maps, iconographic representation of
facilities, and directional information. Notably, the Bristol hubs
included green cubes mounted overhead hub facilities, which appear
likely to assist in finding hub elements in busy urban environments.
Mapping and informational material, however, often appeared to include
some that are not specifically `part' of a hub (e.g.~toilets within
another facilities, shops, cafes). This highlights a potential question
for implementers: what services and facilities to include as `part of
the hub', versus those that are just `nearby'? In the case of the
Transport for West Midlands hub at Andrew Road directional signage to,
and information about, bus services at the nearby Halesowen Bus Station
(approx 50m walking distance) is provided, but the hub has a different
location-based name and branding identity. Some of the hubs included
linemarked areas for shared e-bike, e-scooter or e- cargobike parking,
bicycle parking and secure parking lockers, although the offered
facilities and services varied by location. A lack of shade, weather
protection and drinking water was also evident at most of the visited
hubs, even though many of the parklet-type facilities included steel
framing that might have accommodated open- sided roofing or similar. A
containerised ``Library of Things'' appears to have been added later to
one hub in Bristol, onto top of areas linemarked and sign-posted as for
electric scooter hire. This may provide an indication of the
possibilities of using modular components to expand, re-purpose or
adjust mobility hubs as situations change (consistent with
\citet{Stuart:2002aa}), but also a need for signs and maps to be easily
adjustable to match, once the original graphic designers or implementers
have moved on\footnote{See also \citet{Reynolds:2017ah} discussion of
  `The Garbage Can Model'}.

The site visits perhaps also highlighted the importance of maintenance,
activity\footnote{c.f. \citet{Jacobs:1961aa} ``eyes on the street''} and
updates to match changing circumstances that might give a hub a sense of
`place'. Hubs in Bristol perhaps provide an example with litter,
non-mobility-related signage, space requisitioned for other purposes,
and some sites advertising dockless shared vehicles but without these
present in sufficient numbers (or at all at the time of visiting
Gainsborough Square) all providing indications of potential
post-implementation challenges related to mobility hubs.

\subsection{Definition}\label{definition}

Findings from the sites visits suggest a wide variety of modes, services
and facilities might be provided as part of the hub itself, as well as
further services and facilities identified as being `nearby' as an
extension of the hub offer. This might suggest adopting an open-ended
and expansionist approach to mobility hub definition. The consistency of
branding at the Bristol hubs, especially with respect to the
iconographic green cubes drawing attention to hub elements, might
highlight the importance of proximity and line-of-sight in defining a
mobility hub\footnote{Although mapping at most hubs provided information
  about services and facilities within up to around 15 minutes walking
  distance, hub elements appeared to generally be located within
  relatively confined areas, generally spanning less than 50 metres. The
  Andrew Road hub provides an example towards the upper end, with the
  entirety of the hub and the nearby Halesown Bus Station encompassed
  within a radius of approximately 70 metres, and spanning slightly less
  than 200 metres if traversed by foot. In contrast, the South Woodford
  hub takes up approximately 15 metres of kerb space and is less than
  100 metres from the nearest tube station, while some in Camden use
  space equivalent to only a few car parking spots.}

Definitions of the facilities, services and modes necessary to become a
mobility hub appear likewise to be open-ended and inclusive, rather than
some sort of minimum checklist that all sites must have\footnote{CoMoUK's
  pop-up mobility hub offering provide an example, with the basic option
  consisting of a 12 square metre parklet with bike repair facilitates,
  and bike parking and/or bike share docking \citep{CoMoUK:0ab}}.
\citet{CoMoUK:0ab} discuss six types of mobility hubs: ``large
interchanges/city hubs; transport corridor, smaller interchanges or
linking hubs; business park or new housing development hubs;
suburb/mini-hubs; small market town/village hubs; and tourism hubs''
along with suggested components for each. Further types might include
the `shared' and `electric' forms of mobility hub, as in the ROBUST
sites, wherein the offered modes are available for hire and electrically
powered respectively. Guidance from \citet{CoMoUK:0ab} also suggests: a
timeline of 12 to 15 months to step through consultation, design,
stakeholder engagement, tendering and construction, although this
appears to relate to either unpowered mobility hubs or replaceable
batteries; budgets are in the vicinity of 250k to 650k pounds; and that
``mobility hubs work best when part of a wider sustainable transport
network that provides active travel, public transport and shared
transport options'' \citep{CoMoUK:0ab}.

The design problem here, therefore, appears be to allow creation of
mobility hubs that: incorporate existing modes, facilities and services;
minimise cost and timeframes, such that practitioners and tactical
urbanists might build legitimacy through implementation (c.f.
\citet{Reynolds:2021ab}) using minimal resources, incremental approaches
that allow expansion one-hub-at-a-time, and that are scale-able; and
allow for rapid iteration, revision or adjustment to account for
changing circumstances.

\subsection{Development}\label{development}

Both the Brimingham and Bristol mobility hubs feature pedestal-mounted
signage that includes: the hub name; iconographic representations of hub
facilities; directional indications; and two maps. These maps are,
respectively, of the local vicinity (a 5-10 minute walk) of the hub; and
the surrounding area (a 15-20 minute walk). A starting point for
developing software packages to support creation of further mobility
hubs, therefore, would appear to be outputting similar maps of the local
and surrounding areas, composited together into an image of similar size
to the pedestal signage encountered during the site visits along with
site naming and other information. Figure 2 shows the output of software
developed during this research.

Here the software has been used to create output for the Galway ROBUST
e-mobility hub\footnote{Although as an example the `surrounding area
  map' shown is for the Letterkenny ROBUST hub location so as to
  demonstrate the generalisability of the research outcomes.}. Key
features of the developed software include the ability to highlight
points of interest\footnote{Various religious and medical buildings are
  highlighted in purple, while the library is shown in blue and nearby
  schools in orange.}, as per the maps found in site visits, but might
allow considerable flexibility for practitioners in choosing between
colours, iconography or other symbols. A powerful aspect of adopting the
OSM datasets is that settings can be adjusted by the user and new maps
generated within minutes. For example, the `surrounding area map' shown
in Figure 3 a slightly different OSM tile option is selected, and
buildings recorded as ``pubs'' (rather than public buildings) are shown
in blue. Another flexibility of this approach is that maps at other
scales can be produced, including at small scales which might include
individual hub elements (i.e.~the e-bikes or car share vehicles) so that
users can locate the various services and facilities.

This represents an extension of the mapping approaches seen in the site
visits, and might perhaps remove the need for signage or other physical
elements to mark the location of hub elements (e.g.~the green cubes in
Bristol).

\subsection{Delivery}\label{delivery}

The delivered research outputs are available in two forms. An R markdown
file containing the text of this paper, and the code used to generate
tables, figures and undertake analysis is available at
\url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/WP5_mobilityHubTools_papers/blob/main/ReynoldsEtAl2026_CERC.Rmd}.
This is a static version of the tools as at the time of paper
publication.

As discussed above, this paper is part of a larger program of research
seeking to develop tools for practitioners and mobility hub
implementers. A developmental version is available as an R package at
\url{https://github.com/James-Reynolds/mobilityHubTools}. This is a
non-static version, which is expected to continue to be modified as the
program of research progresses.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{discussion-and-conclusions}

This design-based research has developed basic software tools that might
be used in the context of mobility hubs. The general format and styling
is largely based on elements in place at various mobility hubs in
Bristol, but as discussed above the flexibility of R might allow users
to adjust layouts, styling and other details as desired. As well as
being freely available, the OSM online platform allows this dataset to
be edited by anyone, including for the purposes of mobility hub
implementation Clearly, there are many avenues for further research,
including: expansion to produce websites, posters, banners, leaflets and
other informational products; developing more generic materials for
other purposes such as maintenance schedules, inspection logs,
management resources and other items that might help a mobility hub
`product owner' facilitate operations, continuance and growth. Further
development of `best practices' in mobility hub informational provision
might involve additional case studies, especially of non-UK sites, as
well as improvements to the base `hub signage mockup' developed here.
There may be a need to better understand what facilities and services
might best be included as `part' of a hub, as opposed to those that are
mapped as being `nearby'. More immediately, user testing and further
development of the software, and of the software outputs, especially the
maps, would seem an important direction for further research efforts in
the short term. This paper's title asked a question related to the
applicability of tactical urbanist approaches to the topic of mobility
hubs. Underlying this may be larger questions related to `what is a
mobility hub' and `who gets to define and advertise something as being a
mobility hub'. Perhaps every railway station, bus or tram stop, shared
or micro- mobility facilities or many other places are already mobility
hubs, but just no one has put up a sign or handed out a leaflet that
designates this as being the case? With access to this software and a
printer, turning the existing mobility offerings in your own community
into a defined hub may be within easier reach.
